Lake Tahoe 2013: Word Vectors & Memory Decay

In which Heqing Kwek Lee discovers that scents form continuous vector manifolds, Tomas Mikolov shocks NIPS with vector arithmetic, and recurrent networks suffer from exponential singular value amnesia.

"In December 2013, the machine learning world converged upon Harrah's and MontBleu casinos on the snow-laden shores of Lake Tahoe for NIPS. Downstairs, blackjack dealers shuffled cards under fluorescent chandeliers; upstairs, in the cavernous exhibition halls, the future of artificial intelligence was quietly splitting wide open. The poster sessions were packed shoulder-to-shoulder, but around one poster in particular—Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado, and Jeffrey Dean presenting 'Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases'—the crowd stood six rows deep. For fifty years, symbolic AI and classical NLP had treated human vocabulary as discrete, mutually orthogonal atomic tokens. If you had 50,000 words, you lived in a 50,000-dimensional coordinate space where 'dog' and 'hound' were as totally orthogonal as 'dog' and 'thermodynamics'. But Mikolov showed that by training a shallow two-layer network to predict neighboring words from continuous contexts, words spontaneously organized into a smooth Riemannian manifold where vector arithmetic captured human semantics: \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{King}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{Man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{Woman}} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{Queen}}\). It was linear algebra asserting total dominion over human linguistics."

— From the Memoirs of a Veteran AI Scholar

Act I: The Orthogonality Curse of One-Hot Representations

Let our vocabulary be a finite discrete dictionary \(\mathcal{V} = \{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_{|\mathcal{V}|}\}\). In classical natural language processing, each word \(w_i\) is mapped to the standard canonical basis vector \(\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\):

\[ \mathbf{e}_i = [0, \dots, 0, \underbrace{1}_{i\text{-th entry}}, 0, \dots, 0]^\top \]
Geometric Breakdown: The Metric Collapse of One-Hot Spaces

Consider the standard Euclidean inner productEuclidean Dot ProductThe canonical bilinear form \(\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = \sum_{k=1}^d x_k y_k\), inducing the standard Euclidean norm \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x} \rangle}\). on \(\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\): \[ \langle \mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{e}_j \rangle = \mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{e}_j = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j \end{cases} \]

Now inspect the two primary geometric distance metrics between any two distinct words \(w_i \neq w_j\):

  • 1. Cosine SimilarityCosine SimilarityThe normalized inner product measuring the cosine of the angle between two vectors: \(\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} \in [-1, 1]\). Measures directional alignment independent of magnitude.: \[ \cos \theta(\mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{e}_j) = \frac{\langle \mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{e}_j \rangle}{\|\mathbf{e}_i\|_2 \|\mathbf{e}_j\|_2} = \frac{0}{1 \cdot 1} = 0 \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \; (90^\circ) \]
  • 2. Euclidean Distance: \[ \|\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_j\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|} (e_{i,k} - e_{j,k})^2} = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{2} \]

The Topological Disaster: In this space, the pair \(\{\text{"hound"}, \text{"dog"}\}\) has an inner product of exactly \(0\) and a distance of \(\sqrt{2}\). The pair \(\{\text{"dog"}, \text{"refrigerator"}\}\) has an identical inner product of \(0\) and an identical distance of \(\sqrt{2}\).

Because every word vector is strictly orthogonal to every other word vector, the space has zero metric gradient. A statistical model or neural network that learns a weight update for \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{dog}}\) learns literally nothing about \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{hound}}\). To generalize, we must project these sparse basis vectors into a dense, continuous latent manifold \(\mathbb{R}^d\) where semantic similarity corresponds to geometric proximity.

Act II: Continuous Embeddings & The Softmax Partition Bottleneck

To escape the orthogonality curse, we turn to the Distributional Hypothesis, articulated by Zellig Harris (1954) and John Rupert Firth (1957):

\[ \text{"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."} \]

Instead of representing each word as an isolated basis vector, we embed each word \(w \in \mathcal{V}\) as a dense vector in a low-dimensional Hilbert space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), where \(d \ll |\mathcal{V}|\) (typically \(d \in [100, 300]\)). To capture asymmetric context dynamics, Word2Vec assigns two separate vectors to each token:

  • \(\mathbf{v}_w \in \mathbb{R}^d\): The center target word representation (when the token sits at the center of the sliding window).
  • \(\mathbf{u}_c \in \mathbb{R}^d\): The context representation (when the token appears within a neighborhood radius \(m\) of the center word).

CBOW vs. Skip-Gram Architectures

Mikolov et al. (2013) proposed two complementary architectures:

  1. Continuous Bag-of-Words (CBOW): Takes the average of context word vectors \(\bar{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{-m \leq j \leq m, j \neq 0} \mathbf{u}_{w_{t+j}}\) and predicts the center word \(\mathbf{v}_{w_t}\). Efficient for smaller datasets; smooths over distributional noise.
  2. Skip-Gram: Inverts the direction: takes a single center word \(\mathbf{v}_{w_t}\) and predicts each neighboring context word \(\mathbf{u}_{w_{t+j}}\) within window radius \(m\). Excels at representing rare tokens and subtle semantic distinctions.

The Softmax Partition Bottleneck

Under the Skip-Gram formulation, the probability of observing context word \(c\) given center word \(w\) is modeled via the categorical Softmax functionSoftmax FunctionThe normalized exponential map \(\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}\), mapping real-valued logits to a valid probability distribution on the simplex \(\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}\).:

\[ P(c \mid w) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w)}{\sum_{w' \in \mathcal{V}} \exp(\mathbf{u}_{w'}^\top \mathbf{v}_w)} \]

To train the model, we maximize the log-likelihood across a corpus of length \(T\): \[ \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{-m \leq j \leq m, j \neq 0} \log P(w_{t+j} \mid w_t) \]

The Mathematical Catastrophe: Why Full Softmax is Intractable

Inspect the gradient of \(\log P(c \mid w)\) with respect to the center word vector \(\mathbf{v}_w\):

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_w} \log P(c \mid w) = \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_w} \left( \mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w - \log \sum_{w' \in \mathcal{V}} \exp(\mathbf{u}_{w'}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \right) \]

Applying the chain rule to the second term:

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_w} \log P(c \mid w) = \mathbf{u}_c - \frac{\sum_{w' \in \mathcal{V}} \exp(\mathbf{u}_{w'}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \mathbf{u}_{w'}}{\sum_{w'' \in \mathcal{V}} \exp(\mathbf{u}_{w''}^\top \mathbf{v}_w)} = \mathbf{u}_c - \sum_{w' \in \mathcal{V}} P(w' \mid w) \mathbf{u}_{w'} \]
\[ \boxed{\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_w} \log P(c \mid w) = \mathbf{u}_c - \mathbb{E}_{w' \sim P(\cdot \mid w)} [\mathbf{u}_{w'}]} \]

The Complexity Wall: Look at the expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{w' \sim P(\cdot \mid w)} [\mathbf{u}_{w'}]\). To compute a single gradient update for a single word pair, you must evaluate the dot product \(\mathbf{u}_{w'}^\top \mathbf{v}_w\) and sum across every single word \(w' \in \mathcal{V}\) in the entire vocabulary!

For a modest real-world dictionary where \(|\mathcal{V}| = 100{,}000\) to \(1{,}000{,}000\), computing this normalization constant (the statistical mechanics partition functionPartition Function \(Z\)In thermodynamics and probability, \(Z = \sum_{s} e^{-E(s)/k_B T}\). Calculating the global normalizing denominator over astronomical state spaces makes exact inference intractable. \(Z\)) requires millions of floating-point operations per token. On a corpus of 10 billion words, naive Softmax gradient descent would take years of compute.

Act III: Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) & Step-by-Step Derivation

Tomas Mikolov solved the partition function bottleneck with a stroke of genius: Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS).

Instead of solving a massive \(|\mathcal{V}|\)-way multinomial classification problem at every step, we reframe the task as a collection of independent binary logistic regressions. The network is asked:

\[ \text{"Did the word pair } (w, c) \text{ come from real human text } (\mathcal{D}=1), \text{ or was } c \text{ drawn from random noise } (\mathcal{D}=0)\text{?"} \]
Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation of the SGNS Objective

Using the standard sigmoid function \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\), we model the probability that a pair \((w, c)\) is drawn from genuine text \(\mathcal{D}\):

\[ P(\mathcal{D}=1 \mid w, c) = \sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w)} \]

The probability that a noise word \(c_n\) does not come from the real text is:

\[ P(\mathcal{D}=0 \mid w, c_n) = 1 - P(\mathcal{D}=1 \mid w, c_n) = 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{u}_{c_n}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \]

Recall the fundamental algebraic identity of the logistic sigmoid: \(1 - \sigma(z) = \sigma(-z)\). Thus:

\[ P(\mathcal{D}=0 \mid w, c_n) = \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{c_n}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \]

For each genuine context word \(c\), we sample \(K\) "noise" words \(c_{n_1}, \dots, c_{n_K}\) from a smoothed unigram noise distribution \(P_n(\mathcal{V})\). Assuming statistical independence under the noise generator, the joint likelihood for this sample tuple is:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{tuple}} = P(\mathcal{D}=1 \mid w, c) \cdot \prod_{k=1}^K P(\mathcal{D}=0 \mid w, c_{n_k}) = \sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \prod_{k=1}^K \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \]

Taking the natural logarithm yields the canonical SGNS objective function to be maximized:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}} = \log \sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w) + \sum_{k=1}^K \log \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}^\top \mathbf{v}_w)} \]

The 3/4 Power Noise Smoothing:

How should the noise words \(c_{n_k}\) be sampled? If we sample purely according to raw unigram frequency \(U(w)\), words like "the" and "is" would dominate 99% of negative samples. Mikolov found empirically that raising frequencies to the \(3/4\) power struck an optimal balance between frequent and rare words:

\[ P_n(w) = \frac{U(w)^{3/4}}{\sum_{w' \in \mathcal{V}} U(w')^{3/4}} \]

This subtle power transform amplifies rare words (like "aardvark" or "squirrel") relative to stopwords!

Exact Analytical Gradients & Physical Dynamics

To optimize via gradient ascent, we compute the exact partial derivatives. Recall: \[ \frac{d}{dz} \log \sigma(z) = 1 - \sigma(z), \quad \frac{d}{dz} \log \sigma(-z) = -\sigma(z) \]

Applying the chain rule to \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}\):

  • 1. Gradient with respect to Center Vector \(\mathbf{v}_w\):
    \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_w} \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}} = \left( 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \right) \mathbf{u}_c - \sum_{k=1}^K \sigma(\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}} \]
  • 2. Gradient with respect to True Context Vector \(\mathbf{u}_c\):
    \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{u}_c} \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}} = \left( 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \right) \mathbf{v}_w \]
  • 3. Gradient with respect to Negative Context Vector \(\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}\):
    \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}} = -\sigma(\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \mathbf{v}_w \]

Physical & Geometric Intuition: Two Opposing Physical Forces

Look closely at the gradient vector \(\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_w} \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}\):

  • The first term \(\left( 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \right) \mathbf{u}_c\) acts as a gravitational spring: it pulls \(\mathbf{v}_w\) directly toward the genuine context vector \(\mathbf{u}_c\). The weaker their current alignment (\(\sigma \approx 0\)), the stronger the pull.
  • The second term \(-\sum_{k=1}^K \sigma(\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}^\top \mathbf{v}_w) \mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}\) acts as an electrostatic Coulomb repulsion: it pushes \(\mathbf{v}_w\) directly away from each noise vector \(\mathbf{u}_{c_{n_k}}\). The more dangerously aligned \(\mathbf{v}_w\) accidentally is with a negative sample (\(\sigma \approx 1\)), the stronger the repulsive blast!
Step-by-Step Numerical Walkthrough: 1 Training Step by Hand

Let us trace an exact numerical step on paper in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) so you can see the vector arithmetic in action.

Setup:

  • Center target word: \(w = \text{"dog"}\), initial vector: \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.40 \\ -0.60 \end{bmatrix}\)
  • True context word: \(c = \text{"bark"}\), initial vector: \(\mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.50 \\ 0.20 \end{bmatrix}\)
  • Negative noise sample (\(K=1\)): \(c_n = \text{"cloud"}\), vector: \(\mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.30 \\ 0.70 \end{bmatrix}\)
  • Learning rate: \(\eta = 0.10\)

Step 1: Compute Forward Dot Products & Predictions

  • Positive dot product: \[ z_{\text{pos}} = \mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}}^\top \mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}} = (0.50)(0.40) + (0.20)(-0.60) = 0.20 - 0.12 = 0.08 \] \[ \sigma(z_{\text{pos}}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.08}} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 0.92312} \approx 0.51998 \]
  • Negative dot product: \[ z_{\text{neg}} = \mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}}^\top \mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}} = (-0.30)(0.40) + (0.70)(-0.60) = -0.12 - 0.42 = -0.54 \] \[ \sigma(z_{\text{neg}}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{0.54}} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 1.71601} \approx 0.36819 \] \[ \sigma(-z_{\text{neg}}) = 1 - 0.36819 = 0.63181 \]

Step 2: Compute Current Objective Value

\[ \mathcal{L} = \log(0.51998) + \log(0.63181) = -0.65396 - 0.45917 = -1.11313 \]

Step 3: Compute Exact Gradients

  • Positive residual: \(1 - \sigma(z_{\text{pos}}) = 1 - 0.51998 = 0.48002\)
  • Negative residual: \(-\sigma(z_{\text{neg}}) = -0.36819\)
  • Gradient for \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}}\): \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}}} \mathcal{L} = 0.48002 \begin{bmatrix} 0.50 \\ 0.20 \end{bmatrix} - 0.36819 \begin{bmatrix} -0.30 \\ 0.70 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ = \begin{bmatrix} 0.24001 \\ 0.09600 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -0.11046 \\ 0.25773 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.35047 \\ -0.16173 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Gradient for \(\mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}}\): \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}}} \mathcal{L} = 0.48002 \begin{bmatrix} 0.40 \\ -0.60 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.19201 \\ -0.28801 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Gradient for \(\mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}}\): \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}}} \mathcal{L} = -0.36819 \begin{bmatrix} 0.40 \\ -0.60 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.14728 \\ 0.22091 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 4: Update Parameters via Gradient Ascent

\[ \mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}}^{(\text{new})} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}} + \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_{\text{dog}}} \mathcal{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.40 \\ -0.60 \end{bmatrix} + 0.10 \begin{bmatrix} 0.35047 \\ -0.16173 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.43505 \\ -0.61617 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}}^{(\text{new})} = \mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}} + \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{u}_{\text{bark}}} \mathcal{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.50 \\ 0.20 \end{bmatrix} + 0.10 \begin{bmatrix} 0.19201 \\ -0.28801 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.51920 \\ 0.17120 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}}^{(\text{new})} = \mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}} + \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{u}_{\text{cloud}}} \mathcal{L} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.30 \\ 0.70 \end{bmatrix} + 0.10 \begin{bmatrix} -0.14728 \\ 0.22091 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.31473 \\ 0.72209 \end{bmatrix} \]

Verification: Check the New Dot Products!

  • New alignment with genuine context: \[ z_{\text{pos}}^{(\text{new})} = (0.51920)(0.43505) + (0.17120)(-0.61617) = 0.22588 - 0.10549 = 0.12039 > 0.08000 \quad \checkmark \] The model increased its confidence from \(51.99\%\) to \(53.01\%\)!
  • New alignment with noise: \[ z_{\text{neg}}^{(\text{new})} = (-0.31473)(0.43505) + (0.72209)(-0.61617) = -0.13692 - 0.44493 = -0.58185 < -0.54000 \quad \checkmark \] The negative alignment became more negative—the noise vector was successfully repelled!

Act IV: Why Does Vector Arithmetic Work? (The Geometry of Analogies)

Why did linear vector arithmetic spontaneously discover semantic analogies like:

\[ \mathbf{v}_{\text{King}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{Man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{Woman}} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{Queen}} \]

For a year following NIPS 2013, researchers debated whether this was mathematical alchemy or an artifact of high-dimensional geometry. In 2014, Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg published a landmark paper at NIPS proving that Word2Vec with Negative Sampling is mathematically equivalent to computing the optimal low-rank matrix factorization of the Shifted Pointwise Mutual Information (SPMI) matrix.

Conversations with Heqing Kwek Lee: Canine Olfactory Algebra

In latent space, the vector difference \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{Woman}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{Man}}\) is a directed translation vector representing the gender subspace. When you factorize the global association matrix, linear translations directly mirror relational semantic shifts!"

The Levy & Goldberg (NIPS 2014) Implicit Matrix Factorization Proof

Let us prove this rigorously. Let \(\mathbf{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times |\mathcal{V}|}\) be a matrix whose entries represent the inner products between center word vectors and context word vectors: \[ M_{wc} = \mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w \]

Let \(\#(w, c)\) be the observed count of pair \((w, c)\) in corpus \(\mathcal{D}\), and let \(\#(w) = \sum_{c'} \#(w, c')\) and \(\#(c) = \sum_{w'} \#(w', c)\). Under negative sampling with unigram distribution \(P_n(c) = \frac{\#(c)}{|\mathcal{D}|}\), the expected objective over the entire corpus is the sum of local cell objectives \(\mathcal{J}(M_{wc})\):

\[ \mathcal{J}(\mathbf{M}) = \sum_{w \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{c \in \mathcal{V}} \mathcal{J}(M_{wc}) \] \[ \mathcal{J}(M_{wc}) = \#(w, c) \log \sigma(M_{wc}) + K \cdot \frac{\#(w) \#(c)}{|\mathcal{D}|} \log \sigma(-M_{wc}) \]

To find the global maximum of \(\mathcal{J}(M_{wc})\), let \(x = M_{wc}\) and differentiate with respect to \(x\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{J}(x)}{\partial x} = \#(w, c) \cdot (1 - \sigma(x)) - K \frac{\#(w) \#(c)}{|\mathcal{D}|} \cdot \sigma(x) \]

Setting the derivative to zero (\(\frac{\partial \mathcal{J}}{\partial x} = 0\)):

\[ \#(w, c) \cdot (1 - \sigma(x)) = K \frac{\#(w) \#(c)}{|\mathcal{D}|} \cdot \sigma(x) \]

Dividing both sides by \((1 - \sigma(x))\) and recalling that \(\frac{\sigma(x)}{1 - \sigma(x)} = \frac{1/(1+e^{-x})}{e^{-x}/(1+e^{-x})} = e^x\):

\[ e^x = \frac{\#(w, c)}{K \frac{\#(w) \#(c)}{|\mathcal{D}|}} = \frac{\#(w, c) \cdot |\mathcal{D}|}{\#(w) \#(c)} \cdot \frac{1}{K} = \frac{P(w, c)}{P(w) P(c)} \cdot \frac{1}{K} \]

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

\[ \boxed{x = M_{wc} = \mathbf{u}_c^\top \mathbf{v}_w = \log\left( \frac{P(w, c)}{P(w) P(c)} \right) - \log K = \text{PMI}(w, c) - \log K} \]

The Profound Conclusion: Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling does not perform mystical alchemy. It is an implicit, stochastic algorithm for calculating the low-rank Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the Shifted Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) matrix!

GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation (Pennington et al., 2014)

Recognizing this implicit matrix factorization, Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher, and Christopher Manning (EMNLP 2014) asked: why use noisy local sampling to approximate a global matrix when you can fit global co-occurrences directly?

They proposed GloVe, an explicit log-bilinear regression model on the global co-occurrence matrix \(\mathbf{X}\). GloVe minimizes the weighted least-squares loss:

\[ \mathcal{J}_{\text{GloVe}} = \sum_{i,j=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|} f(X_{ij}) \left( \mathbf{w}_i^\top \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log X_{ij} \right)^2 \]

where the weighting function \(f(x)\) prevents extremely frequent words (like "the") or rare noisy co-occurrences from dominating:

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} \left( \frac{x}{x_{\max}} \right)^\alpha & \text{if } x < x_{\max} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \quad (\text{typically } x_{\max} = 100, \, \alpha = 0.75) \]

At each time step \(t\), the network takes the current word vector \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and updates its internal hidden state: \(\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{hh} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h)\)."

Act VI: Sequential Memory & The Tragic Fate of RNNs

Let a recurrent neural network process a sequence of \(T\) discrete tokens \(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_T\). The discrete-time dynamical system evolves according to:

\[ \mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hh} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h \] \[ \mathbf{h}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{z}_t) = \tanh(\mathbf{z}_t) \]

Suppose a loss \(\mathcal{L}_T\) is computed at final step \(T\). How does this error backpropagate across time to modify the weights governing the initial hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_1\)?

Formal Proof of Gradient Vanishing via Spectral Analysis (Bengio 1994, Pascanu 2013)

By the multivariable chain rule (known as Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)The unrolling of a recurrent network across \(T\) time steps into an equivalent feedforward DAG, allowing standard reverse-mode automatic differentiation to accumulate weight gradients across the temporal sequence.):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_1} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T} \cdot \prod_{k=2}^T \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{k-1}} \]

Differentiating \(\mathbf{h}_k = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{hh} \mathbf{h}_{k-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh} \mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{b}_h)\) with respect to \(\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{k-1}} = \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top \]

where \(\mathbf{D}_k = \text{diag}\left( \tanh'(\mathbf{z}_k) \right) = \text{diag}\left( 1 - \tanh^2(\mathbf{z}_k) \right)\) is a diagonal matrix containing the local derivatives of the hyperbolic tangent.

The cumulative temporal Jacobian matrix from step \(1\) to step \(T\) is:

\[ \mathbf{J}_{1 \to T} = \prod_{k=2}^T \left( \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top \right) = \left( \mathbf{D}_T \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top \right) \left( \mathbf{D}_{T-1} \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top \right) \dots \left( \mathbf{D}_2 \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top \right) \]

Now, evaluate the matrix \(2\)-norm (spectral norm, defined as the largest singular value \(\sigma_{\max}\)): Recall the submultiplicative property: \(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_2 \leq \|\mathbf{A}\|_2 \|\mathbf{B}\|_2\).

\[ \left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_1} \right\|_2 \leq \left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T} \right\|_2 \prod_{k=2}^T \|\mathbf{D}_k\|_2 \|\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\|_2 \]

Inspect the two factors:

  1. Activation Derivative: For \(\tanh(z)\), the derivative satisfies \(\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z) \leq 1\). Therefore, \(\|\mathbf{D}_k\|_2 \leq 1\) everywhere (and if the neuron saturates, \(\|\mathbf{D}_k\|_2 \to 0\)).
  2. Weight Spectral Norm: Let \(\lambda_1 = \|\mathbf{W}_{hh}\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{W}_{hh})\) be the maximum singular valueSingular Values & Spectral RadiusThe singular values \(\sigma_i\) of a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) are the square roots of the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A}\). The largest singular value \(\sigma_{\max}\) bounds the maximum amplification factor \(\sup_{\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}} \frac{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}\). of the recurrent weight matrix.

Combining these bounds:

\[ \boxed{\left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_1} \right\|_2 \leq \left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T} \right\|_2 \cdot (\lambda_1)^{T-1}} \]

The Mathematical Verdict: Two Disasters

  • Disaster 1: Vanishing Gradients (\(\lambda_1 < 1\)): If the largest singular value is strictly less than 1 (say \(\lambda_1 = 0.9\)), then across a modest sentence of \(T = 50\) tokens: \[ (0.9)^{49} \approx 0.0057 \quad (\text{over 99.4\% of the gradient signal vanishes}) \] For \(T = 100\), \((0.9)^{99} \approx 2.95 \times 10^{-5}\). Gradients underflow into machine precision zero! The network physically cannot learn long-range causal dependencies.
  • Disaster 2: Exploding Gradients (\(\lambda_1 > 1\)): If \(\lambda_1 = 1.2\), then: \[ (1.2)^{49} \approx 7{,}580, \quad (1.2)^{99} \approx 6.9 \times 10^7 \] The gradient explodes exponentially, destabilizing training into IEEE floating-point NaN (Not-a-Number) overflows.

The Band-Aid: Gradient Clipping

Mikolov (2012) and Pascanu et al. (2013) mitigated exploding gradients by introducing norm clipping:

\[ \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \begin{cases} \mathbf{g} & \text{if } \|\mathbf{g}\|_2 \leq \gamma \\ \frac{\gamma}{\|\mathbf{g}\|_2} \mathbf{g} & \text{if } \|\mathbf{g}\|_2 > \gamma \end{cases} \]

While clipping successfully tames explosions, it is completely powerless against vanishing gradients—you cannot rescale a vector whose magnitude has already decayed to zero!

Act VII: The Gated Highway — LSTMs & GRUs

How do we prevent gradients from vanishing when multiplied through time? In 1997, Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber published the architectural breakthrough: the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM).

The core mathematical insight of the LSTM is to separate memory into two channels:

  • \(\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\): The Hidden State (fast-changing, non-linear working memory).
  • \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\): The Cell State (slow-changing, linear internal storage highway).
The Complete Mathematical Formulation of LSTM

At time step \(t\), given input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and previous hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\), the LSTM evaluates six equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_f \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_f) &&\text{(1. Forget Gate: What fraction of past memory to retain)} \\ \mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_i \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_i) &&\text{(2. Input Gate: What fraction of new features to admit)} \\ \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t &= \tanh(\mathbf{W}_c \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_c \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_c) &&\text{(3. Candidate State: New raw information extracted from } \mathbf{x}_t) \\ \mathbf{C}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t &&\text{(4. Cell State: Linear additive update — The Gradient Highway!)} \\ \mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_o \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_o) &&\text{(5. Output Gate: What part of cell state to reveal)} \\ \mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{C}_t) &&\text{(6. Hidden State: Filtered working memory exposed to world)} \end{aligned} \]
The Proof: The Additive Gradient Highway (\(\frac{\partial \mathbf{C}_t}{\partial \mathbf{C}_{t-1}} \approx \mathbf{I}\))

Why does the LSTM solve vanishing gradients? Differentiate equation (4) with respect to the previous cell state \(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{C}_t}{\partial \mathbf{C}_{t-1}} = \text{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t) + \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \odot \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}_t}{\partial \mathbf{C}_{t-1}} + \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t \odot \frac{\partial \mathbf{i}_t}{\partial \mathbf{C}_{t-1}} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t}{\partial \mathbf{C}_{t-1}} \]

Because the gates \(\mathbf{f}_t, \mathbf{i}_t, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t\) depend on \(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\) only indirectly through the previous hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\), the dominant term is simply:

\[ \boxed{\frac{\partial \mathbf{C}_t}{\partial \mathbf{C}_{t-1}} \approx \text{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t)} \]

Notice the radical difference from the vanilla RNN!

  • In the vanilla RNN, the Jacobian \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top\) is forced to multiply across weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\) at every single step.
  • In the LSTM, if the network learns to keep the forget gate open (\(\mathbf{f}_t \approx \mathbf{1}\)), the Jacobian is literally the Identity Matrix \(\mathbf{I}\)!

The gradient travels backwards along the cell state highway: \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{C}_1} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{C}_T} \prod_{t=2}^T \text{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t) \approx \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{C}_T} \cdot \mathbf{I} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{C}_T} \]

Gradients flow across hundreds of time steps with strictly zero decay. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber called this the Constant Error Carousel (CEC).

Vanilla RNN (Elman 1990) 0 gates 1 (\(\mathbf{h}_t\)) \(d^2 + d \cdot d_{\text{in}} + d\) None (Multiplicative \(\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top \to\) Vanishing/Exploding)

GRU (Cho et al. 2014) 2 gates (\(\mathbf{z}\) update, \(\mathbf{r}\) reset) 1 (\(\mathbf{h}_t\)) \(3 \times (d^2 + d \cdot d_{\text{in}} + d)\) Coupled interpolation \(\mathbf{h}_t = (1 - \mathbf{z}_t) \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t\)

1. Complete Word2Vec SGNS Module with Negative Sampling

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class Word2VecSGNS(nn.Module):
    """
    Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (Mikolov et al., NIPS 2013).
    Implements binary logistic regression with dual embedding tables.
    """
    def __init__(self, vocab_size: int, embed_dim: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.vocab_size = vocab_size
        self.embed_dim = embed_dim
        
        # Center word vectors (v_w) and context word vectors (u_c)
        self.v_embeddings = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, embed_dim)
        self.u_embeddings = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, embed_dim)
        
        # Uniform initialization scaled by embedding dimension
        bound = 0.5 / embed_dim
        nn.init.uniform_(self.v_embeddings.weight, -bound, bound)
        nn.init.zeros_(self.u_embeddings.weight)

    def forward(self, center_words: torch.Tensor, context_words: torch.Tensor, neg_samples: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        center_words:  (batch_size,)
        context_words: (batch_size,)
        neg_samples:   (batch_size, K)
        """
        # (B, D)
        v_w = self.v_embeddings(center_words)
        # (B, D)
        u_c = self.u_embeddings(context_words)
        # (B, K, D)
        u_neg = self.u_embeddings(neg_samples)
        
        # Positive term: log sigma(u_c^T * v_w)
        pos_dot = torch.sum(v_w * u_c, dim=-1)  # (B,)
        pos_loss = F.logsigmoid(pos_dot)
        
        # Negative term: sum_{k=1}^K log sigma(-u_{n_k}^T * v_w)
        # Batch matrix multiplication: (B, K, D) x (B, D, 1) -> (B, K, 1) -> (B, K)
        neg_dot = torch.bmm(u_neg, v_w.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
        neg_loss = F.logsigmoid(-neg_dot).sum(dim=-1)  # (B,)
        
        # Total Negative Log-Likelihood to minimize via gradient descent
        return -(pos_loss + neg_loss).mean()

    def get_embedding(self, word_idx: int) -> torch.Tensor:
        """Returns the final representation (often v_w + u_w as recommended by Mikolov)."""
        return (self.v_embeddings.weight[word_idx] + self.u_embeddings.weight[word_idx]).detach()

2. Explicit LSTM Cell from Scratch (No nn.LSTM)

class ExplicitLSTMCell(nn.Module):
    """
    Long Short-Term Memory Cell (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997).
    Computes forget, input, candidate, and output gates explicitly.
    """
    def __init__(self, input_dim: int, hidden_dim: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.input_dim = input_dim
        self.hidden_dim = hidden_dim
        
        # Single fused affine projection for computational efficiency:
        # Computes [W_f, W_i, W_c, W_o] in one matrix multiplication
        self.weight_ih = nn.Linear(input_dim, 4 * hidden_dim)
        self.weight_hh = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, 4 * hidden_dim)
        
        # Initialize forget gate bias to 1.0 (Jozefowicz et al., ICML 2015 trick to prevent early vanishing)
        with torch.no_grad():
            self.weight_ih.bias.zero_()
            self.weight_hh.bias.zero_()
            self.weight_hh.bias[0:hidden_dim].fill_(1.0)  # f-gate bias

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor, state: tuple[torch.Tensor, torch.Tensor]) -> tuple[torch.Tensor, torch.Tensor]:
        h_prev, c_prev = state  # Both of shape (B, hidden_dim)
        
        # Project inputs and recurrent states: (B, 4 * hidden_dim)
        gates = self.weight_ih(x) + self.weight_hh(h_prev)
        
        # Split into the 4 constituent gates
        f_gate, i_gate, c_cand, o_gate = gates.chunk(4, dim=-1)
        
        f = torch.sigmoid(f_gate)  # Forget gate
        i = torch.sigmoid(i_gate)  # Input gate
        c_tilde = torch.tanh(c_cand)  # Candidate state
        o = torch.sigmoid(o_gate)  # Output gate
        
        # Additive Constant Error Carousel (CEC)
        c_next = f * c_prev + i * c_tilde
        # Filtered hidden state output
        h_next = o * torch.tanh(c_next)
        
        return h_next, c_next
